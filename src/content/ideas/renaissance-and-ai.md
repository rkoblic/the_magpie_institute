**Lessons from the Renaissance for the AI Age**

In the mid-15th century, Gutenberg’s printing press enabled the mass production of texts, which enabled self-learning at a scale never available in history. The machine emerged in the midst of Renaissance Humanism, further accelerating the cultural movement. As such, the civic project of the era had to shift from the acquisition of knowledge to the formation of individuals who could wield that knowledge. And the study of classical works, grammar, rhetoric, history, poetry, and moral philosophy became all the more essential to the social fabric of the time. 

We are in an analogous moment. 

Artificial intelligence has made the development of knowledge itself radically abundant. The question, now, is what kind of human is capable of wielding such knowledge? The civic challenge of our time, like that of the fifteenth century, is fundamentally one of **formation.** 

MIT’s Sloan School published an article claiming that [Philosophy eats AI](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/philosophy-eats-ai/?utm_term=&utm_campaign=MITSMR+-+PerformanceMax&utm_source=adwords&utm_medium=ppc&hsa_acc=8631786989&hsa_cam=21901598582&hsa_grp=&hsa_ad=&hsa_src=x&hsa_tgt=&hsa_kw=&hsa_mt=&hsa_net=adwords&hsa_ver=3&gad_source=1&gad_campaignid=21895150707&gbraid=0AAAAAD_XyAtlUpCWDXOk5TYq0ITMUKN0g&gclid=CjwKCAjw46HPBhAMEiwASZpLRPASqBDzsoioo0heS-r9qlUtgH5I5UIj5SVyeQIAu_zkCr_ltLxW5hoCar8QAvD_BwE) and that: 

*As a discipline, data set, and sensibility, philosophy increasingly determines how digital technologies reason, predict, create, generate, and innovate. The critical enterprise challenge is whether leaders will possess the self-awareness and rigor to use philosophy as a resource for creating value with AI or default to tacit, unarticulated philosophical principles for their AI deployments. “*

Philosophy, the love of wisdom, has recentered into leadership discourse brought on, we believe, by the abundance of knowledge that is available today and accelerating. 

Petrarch, known as the Father of Renaissance Humanism, wrote in the 14th century that:

*"And what is the use of knowing many things if, when you have learned the dimensions of heaven and earth, the measure of the seas, the courses of stars, the virtues of plants and stones, the secrets of nature, you still don't know yourself?"*

Echoing Petrarch (whether he knows it or not, I don’t know), Eric Oliver at the University of Chicago hosts a “legendary” course called the Intelligible Self embedded within the Political Science Department of all places. His book [*How to Know Your Self*](https://jericoliver.com/how-to-know-your-self/) draws across disciplines to wonder about who we are:

*With warmth, wit, and an eye for the unexpected, Oliver draws on insights from neuroscience, psychology, physics, and ancient philosophy to explore the mystery of the self. What is this thing we call “me”? How does it take shape? Oliver tackles these questions and shows us that the answers we’ve inherited—and even the ones we’ve come to ourselves—are often more illusion than truth.*

That invitation to the development of deep character and understanding of the human person continues to echo over nearly 700 years of history to today. Other lessons from that era resound, too: 

**Pedagogy over Technique:** Pierpaolo Vergerio's *De Ingenuis Moribus* (1403) — argued for a new kind of education that would cultivate people capable of thinking clearly, speaking persuasively, and participating meaningfully in civic life. The humanists understood that a society awash in knowledge needed people formed by something more than literacy alone. It needed people practiced in good judgment.

**Primacy of Ethical and Moral Disciplines:** The *studia humanitatis* taught rhetoric — the art of persuasion and discernment — because the humanists, following Cicero, believed that eloquence joined to wisdom was the highest civic good. It taught history and moral philosophy because practical wisdom could not be generated from first principles; it had to be formed through encounter with life, exemplary people, and the accumulated experience of civilization.

Whereas the printing press allowed for the distribution of knowledge, AI has the ability to actually converse with us and actively think *with* us. Of course there are dangers associated with this especially if we’re not thinking critically or discerning in our conversations. Nevertheless, AI could become a modern *umanisti* (teacher of the humanities) under the right conditions. 

So what could a contemporary *studia humanitatis* look like? Very similar to the questions and themes raised in the Renaissance. 

**Rhetoric** — the art of evaluating arguments, detecting manipulation, and communicating with integrity in an environment saturated by machine-generated language.

**History** — the cultivation of temporal depth, analogical reasoning, and civilizational context. 

**Moral philosophy** — the discipline of deliberating about ends, means, reality, and the highest good. Formation to exercise wisdom in uncertainty and sustaining an unautomatable character. 

**Narrative and poetry —** Irreplaceable training in matters of the human spirit, guidance for ambiguity, and to make meaning from the most important human realities that resist reduction to data.

The main difference, though, will be in how those themes are taught, experienced, and embodied in the human person to such a degree that it is not simply knowledge but the formation of judgement at a fundamental level.
